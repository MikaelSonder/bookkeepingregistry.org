Executive Summary

Bookkeeping in Hong Kong is the structured function that converts business events into reliable accounting records, supporting documents and financial statements. The Companies Ordinance requires each Hong Kong company to keep accounting records sufficient to show and explain its transactions, disclose with reasonable accuracy its financial position and financial performance at any time, and enable directors to ensure that financial statements comply with the statutory framework.

In professional practice, Hong Kong bookkeeping is not merely data entry. It is an ongoing compliance process involving source-document collection, daily entries for money received and expended, sales and purchase invoice control, bank reconciliation, receivables and payables, payroll and MPF information, asset and liability records, year-end adjustments and preparation of financial statements for the annual audit and profits-tax return process.

Hong Kong has a strong audit and tax-record culture. All Hong Kong companies normally prepare annual financial statements and are subject to audit by a Hong Kong practising certified public accountant, subject to specific exemptions and circumstances. The annual audit is typically required to support the Profits Tax Return submitted to the Inland Revenue Department. Private companies usually do not file their financial statements with the Companies Registry, but must keep proper accounts and retain the financial statements and supporting accounting records.

Both company law and tax law establish seven-year retention obligations, but their reference points differ. A company preserves accounting records, accounts and returns for seven years after the end of the financial year to which the final entry or matter relates. Under section 51C of the Inland Revenue Ordinance, persons carrying on a trade, profession or business retain sufficient English or Chinese income-and-expenditure records for at least seven years after completion of the relevant transactions, acts or operations.

The purpose of the bookkeeping function is to ensure that business transactions in Hong Kong are recorded, documented and organised correctly, on time and in a way that supports Companies Ordinance compliance, annual audit, Inland Revenue profits-tax reporting and reliable financial statements.

Country Characteristics

Country characteristics explain the jurisdiction-specific features that shape how bookkeeping operates in Hong Kong. The section matters because bookkeeping is defined not only by arithmetic, but also by regulatory structure, documentation culture and institutional expectations.

Operational Culture Hong Kong bookkeeping is audit-oriented, evidence-driven and closely linked to annual financial statements, profits-tax compliance, English or Chinese record keeping, international trade documentation and cross-border group finance.
Legal Framework Orientation The Companies Ordinance establishes company accounting-record and financial-statement obligations, while the Inland Revenue Ordinance establishes business record requirements for profits-tax purposes. Hong Kong Financial Reporting Standards provide the financial-reporting framework for companies.
Audit Characteristic Annual audit is a central operating feature for Hong Kong companies. Proper bookkeeping must produce schedules, vouchers, bank evidence, contracts and reconciliations capable of supporting audit by a Hong Kong practising certified public accountant and the related profits-tax process.
Record Location and Retention Records can be kept in Hong Kong or elsewhere, but where accounting records are maintained outside Hong Kong, accounts and returns sufficient to show the business’s financial position with reasonable accuracy must be sent to and retained in Hong Kong at intervals not exceeding six months. Seven-year company-law and tax-law retention applies.

Regulatory & Operational Framework

The regulatory and operational framework identifies the principal rule layers that define Hong Kong bookkeeping practice. The section is broader than legislation alone because bookkeeping depends on company law, profits-tax rules, financial-reporting standards, audit requirements, documentation routines and operational procedures.

Companies Ordinance, Section 373 Requires a company to keep accounting records sufficient to show and explain its transactions, disclose its financial position and performance with reasonable accuracy at any time and enable directors to ensure financial statements comply with the Companies Ordinance.
Daily Entries and Asset-Liability Records Accounting records include daily entries of money received and expended, matters to which the receipts and payments relate, and a record of the company’s assets and liabilities. These records form the primary working evidence behind financial statements and audit schedules.
Companies Ordinance Record Location and Retention Accounting records are kept at the registered office or another location selected by the directors. If kept outside Hong Kong, sufficient accounts and returns must be sent to and retained in Hong Kong at intervals of no more than six months. Records are generally preserved for seven years after the relevant financial year.
Inland Revenue Ordinance, Section 51C Requires persons carrying on a trade, profession or business in Hong Kong to keep sufficient English or Chinese records of income and expenditure so assessable profits can be readily ascertained. Records are retained for at least seven years after completion of the relevant transactions, acts or operations.
Financial Statements and Audit Companies prepare financial statements for each financial year, including a statement of financial position, statement of profit or loss, cash-flow statement, statement of changes in equity and notes as applicable. Annual audit is generally conducted by a Hong Kong practising certified public accountant, subject to statutory exemptions and entity circumstances.
Profits-Tax Reporting Bookkeeping records and audited financial statements support Profits Tax Returns, tax computations, deductions, allowance claims and responses to Inland Revenue Department enquiries. The company’s audit and tax cycle commonly depend on complete and reconciled annual accounts.
Process Flow

The process flow explains how bookkeeping work usually progresses from raw transaction to completed records and reporting support. It matters because bookkeeping is an operating sequence, not a single event.

1. Source Document Collection Collect sales and purchase invoices, receipts, bank statements, contracts, delivery records, trade documents, payroll outputs, MPF records, asset evidence, inventory information and other supporting documents for each business transaction.
2. Classification and Profits-Tax Review Classify income, purchases, expenses, payroll, assets, liabilities and other events to appropriate accounts and determine their profits-tax, financial-reporting and audit treatment where applicable.
3. Daily and Journal Recording Record business events in chronological journals and accounting records, including daily cash and bank entries, with clear descriptions, account coding, document references and links to reliable supporting evidence.
4. General and Subsidiary Ledger Maintenance Maintain the general ledger and subsidiary ledgers for receivables, payables, cash, banks, fixed assets, inventory, payroll, MPF, intercompany balances and other accounting information needed to explain transactions and balances.
5. Reconciliation Reconcile bank accounts, receivables, payables, cash, payroll and MPF liabilities, assets, inventory, intercompany accounts and other material balance-sheet accounts to establish accuracy and completeness.
6. Year-End Closing and Audit Preparation Perform period-end procedures and adjustment entries, prepare schedules for annual financial statements and organise audit evidence, tax computations and reconciliations for review by the company’s auditor.
7. Financial Statements and Tax Support Provide final figures, schedules, accounting records and supporting materials for financial statements, annual audit, Profits Tax Returns, company records and IRD review where applicable.
Typical Outputs Journals, general ledger, subsidiary ledgers, trial balance, bank reconciliations, receivable and payable schedules, fixed-asset and inventory records, payroll and MPF records, financial statements, audit schedules, tax computations and retained source documents.
Decision Tree

The decision tree simplifies threshold questions that commonly determine the correct bookkeeping route. It is presented as a logical workflow so that the reader can follow the sequence as an operational progression rather than as a table of detached items.

  1. Identify the business event: sale, purchase, cash movement, payroll output, MPF contribution, asset transaction, inventory movement, import-export event, intercompany charge, adjustment or correction.
  2. Confirm whether the event belongs to a Hong Kong company, registered non-Hong Kong company, branch, permanent establishment or business activity subject to Hong Kong company or profits-tax requirements. If yes, proceed under Hong Kong requirements; if no, assess other jurisdictions or consolidation-only treatment.
  3. Check whether sufficient English or Chinese source evidence exists, including invoices, receipts, contracts, bank records and trade documentation, to explain and substantiate the transaction. If not, resolve the documentation gap before recording.
  4. Assign the event to the appropriate accounts and record it in chronological accounting records, ensuring daily cash and bank information and asset-liability records are maintained as required.
  5. Assess whether the item has cross-border, foreign-currency, intercompany, transfer-pricing, source-of-profits, group-reporting or foreign-company branch elements. If yes, coordinate with Hong Kong accountants, tax advisers, auditors and group finance where necessary.
  6. Reconcile and retain the transaction, include it correctly in financial statements, annual audit, Profits Tax Return support and the applicable seven-year company-law and Inland Revenue record-retention processes.

Cross-Border Relevance

Cross-border relevance explains why bookkeeping in Hong Kong cannot be understood only as a domestic record-keeping process. International group structures, foreign ownership and multi-jurisdiction operations often trigger parallel bookkeeping questions.

Recognition Hong Kong bookkeeping obligations may arise where a Hong Kong company, registered non-Hong Kong company, branch, permanent establishment, profits-tax exposure, local trading activity or other material business connection exists in Hong Kong.
Foreign Companies Foreign-owned Hong Kong companies and registered non-Hong Kong companies need accounting records that support Companies Ordinance, annual audit and Inland Revenue obligations while allowing group reporting in other standards and currencies. Foreign-company annual filings can require head-office and branch financial statements.
Applicable International Rules Bookkeeping may need to interface with Hong Kong Financial Reporting Standards, IFRS-based or US-GAAP group reporting, foreign-currency translation, transfer pricing, tax treaty considerations, source-of-profits analysis, import-export records and intercompany reporting, even though Hong Kong local obligations remain the baseline.
Language and Location Considerations Business records for profits-tax purposes may be kept in English or Chinese. Records can be held outside Hong Kong, but the company must send to and retain in Hong Kong, at intervals not exceeding six months, accounts and returns sufficient to show the financial position of the business with reasonable accuracy.
Typical Cross-Border Scenario A foreign group establishes a Hong Kong private company, registers as a non-Hong Kong company or begins Hong Kong trading activity; local bookkeeping supports accounting records, annual audit, profits-tax compliance and statutory financial statements and is then reconciled to group accounts.
Common Risk Assuming that overseas group accounting, foreign invoices or offshore records alone are sufficient, without maintaining English or Chinese records, Hong Kong audit evidence, local financial-position information, profits-tax support and seven-year retention controls.
Practical Consideration Cross-border bookkeeping often requires coordination between Hong Kong bookkeepers and accountants, practising certified public accountants, tax advisers, company secretaries, group finance and auditors to align local accounts, audit documentation and international reporting.
Key Takeaways
  • Cross-border bookkeeping questions often begin when a foreign entity establishes a Hong Kong company, registers a non-Hong Kong company or begins Hong Kong business activity.
  • Group accounting standards do not replace Hong Kong Companies Ordinance records, annual audit evidence, Inland Revenue profits-tax documentation or local record-location controls.
  • Coordination between Hong Kong local accounting processes and international group finance is essential for compliant reporting, tax support, audit readiness and reliable consolidation.
Operating Constraints Risks

Operating constraints identify the limits, risks and recurring friction points that affect bookkeeping execution in practice.

Documentation Risk Missing, incomplete or unreliable invoices, receipts, contracts, trade documents, bank evidence, payroll records or inventory information can undermine accounting records, profits-tax positions, financial statements and audit defence.
Audit Readiness Risk Late reconciliations, unsupported balances, incomplete schedules, unverified related-party transactions or absent voucher trails can delay the annual audit and weaken the basis for Profits Tax Return preparation.
Record Location and Retention Risk Keeping all records only outside Hong Kong without providing locally retained accounts and returns at the required intervals, or applying only a group-retention policy, can fail to meet Hong Kong company-law and Inland Revenue record-keeping requirements.
Cross-Border Risk Foreign-owned entities may underestimate Hong Kong requirements when relying mainly on overseas finance teams, group ledgers, foreign-language records or systems without Hong Kong audit, profits-tax and local record-location controls.

FAQ

The FAQ section collects recurring threshold questions in a concise handbook format.

Must a Company Keep Accounting Records in Hong Kong? Yes. A company keeps accounting records sufficient to show and explain its transactions, disclose its financial position and performance with reasonable accuracy and enable directors to ensure statutory financial statements comply with the Companies Ordinance.
In Which Language May Business Records Be Kept? For Inland Revenue purposes, persons carrying on a trade, profession or business keep sufficient income-and-expenditure records in English or Chinese so assessable profits can be readily ascertained.
How Long Must Records Be Retained? Company accounting records, accounts and returns are generally preserved for seven years after the relevant financial year. Inland Revenue business records are retained for at least seven years after completion of the relevant transactions, acts or operations.
Does Bookkeeping Support Annual Audit and Profits Tax? Yes. Complete and reconciled accounting records support annual financial statements, audit schedules, Profits Tax Returns, tax computations, deductions and responses to Inland Revenue Department enquiries.
Can a Foreign Company Have Bookkeeping Obligations in Hong Kong? Yes. Foreign-owned Hong Kong entities, registered non-Hong Kong companies, branches, permanent establishments and businesses with Hong Kong profits-tax or local operating activity can have local accounting, audit, financial-statement and record-retention obligations.
