Executive Summary

Bookkeeping in Japan is the structured function that converts business events into reliable accounting books, supporting materials and financial statements. Under the Companies Act, a stock company prepares accounting books and materials important to its business in a timely manner. These accounting books form the basis for the company’s financial statements, supplementary schedules and business reports and are central to management, shareholder, creditor, audit and tax functions.

In professional practice, Japanese bookkeeping is not merely data entry. It is an ongoing compliance process involving invoices and receipt control, journal entries, general-ledger maintenance, cash and bank reconciliation, consumption-tax coding, payroll accounting, asset and inventory controls, closing procedures and preparation of financial statements. Corporate-tax record keeping operates alongside company-law obligations, particularly for corporations filing Blue Form returns that must maintain prescribed books and documentary evidence.

Japan has overlapping retention rules. A stock company retains accounting books and important business materials for ten years from the closing of its books. Financial statements and annexed detailed statements are retained for ten years from their preparation. Corporate-tax books and documents are generally retained for seven years, while records related to loss years and carried-forward losses commonly require ten-year retention. The prudent operational standard for a Japanese company is therefore to map each document category to the longer applicable company-law, corporate-tax, consumption-tax or other regulatory period.

Electronic record preservation is a major practical feature. The Companies Act permits books and financial statements to be created and retained as electromagnetic records. However, electronic transaction data must be preserved in a compliant, searchable and verifiable manner under the Electronic Books Preservation Act and associated National Tax Agency rules. This is especially significant for companies receiving electronic invoices, digital receipts, email-based contracts and data from overseas group systems.

Purpose

The purpose of the bookkeeping function is to ensure that business transactions in Japan are recorded, documented and organised correctly, on time and in a way that supports company-law compliance, corporate-tax and consumption-tax reporting, reliable financial statements and transparent business administration.

It exists to convert legal and commercial obligations into traceable accounting books and supporting materials with clear audit trails and predictable reporting outcomes.

Accurate and timely bookkeeping execution in Japan, including complete accounting books, reliable supporting materials, compliant electronic transaction storage, support for corporate and consumption-tax reporting and robust input for financial statements, business reports and audit.

Regulatory & Operational Framework

The regulatory and operational framework identifies the principal rule layers that define Japanese bookkeeping practice. The section is broader than legislation alone because bookkeeping depends on company law, tax and consumption-tax rules, electronic-document controls, documentation routines and operational procedures.

Companies Act Accounting Books A stock company prepares accounting books and important materials regarding its business in a timely manner. These books provide the information necessary to prepare financial statements and support company governance, shareholder information, audit and statutory compliance.
Companies Act Financial Statements A stock company prepares financial statements and supplementary schedules for each business year. Financial statements include the balance sheet, profit and loss statement, statement of changes in net assets and notes, with related business reports where applicable.
Corporate Tax Act and Blue Form Records Corporate taxpayers maintain books and documents recording transactions for tax purposes. Blue Form taxpayers require proper books and supporting documents, generally retained for seven years, with extended retention commonly applying to records relevant to carried-forward tax losses.
Consumption Tax Records Consumption-tax compliance depends on properly maintained transaction records, tax invoices or qualified invoice records where applicable, purchase evidence, sales evidence and reconciliation between accounting books and tax-reporting positions.
Electronic Books Preservation Act Provides the framework for electronic preservation of books, scanned documents and electronic transaction data. Businesses need controls for data integrity, searchability, retrievability and reliable storage when accounting documents are received or created electronically.
Record Storage and Retention Stock companies retain accounting books and important business materials for ten years from closing the books and retain financial statements and supplementary schedules for ten years from preparation. Corporate-tax records generally follow seven-year retention, with ten-year retention for specified loss-related records and the longer applicable rule used in practice.
Process Flow

The process flow explains how bookkeeping work usually progresses from raw transaction to completed records and reporting support. It matters because bookkeeping is an operating sequence, not a single event.

1. Source Document Collection Collect issued and received invoices, receipts, bank statements, contracts, delivery records, payroll outputs, consumption-tax evidence, asset records, inventory information and electronic transaction data for each business event.
2. Classification and Consumption-Tax Review Classify sales, purchases, expenses, payroll, assets, liabilities and other events to appropriate accounts and determine their consumption-tax, corporate-tax and financial-reporting treatment where applicable.
3. Journal Entry Record business events chronologically in the journal using double-entry accounting, with clear descriptions, account coding, source-document references and links to reliable supporting evidence.
4. General and Subsidiary Ledger Posting Post entries systematically to the general ledger and maintain subsidiary ledgers for receivables, payables, cash, banks, fixed assets, inventory, payroll, consumption tax and intercompany balances.
5. Electronic Data Preservation Preserve electronic transaction data, including digital invoices, receipts, contracts and order information, through controlled storage that supports integrity, searchability, timely retrieval and tax inspection under the electronic-preservation framework.
6. Reconciliation and Period Closing Reconcile bank accounts, receivables, payables, consumption-tax accounts, payroll liabilities, assets, inventory and other material balances; complete adjustment entries and tax-support schedules.
7. Financial Statements and Tax Support Provide final figures, schedules, accounting books and supporting materials for financial statements, business reports, corporate-tax and consumption-tax filings, statutory audit and NTA review where applicable.
Typical Outputs Journal, general ledger, subsidiary ledgers, trial balance, consumption-tax workpapers, bank reconciliations, asset and inventory records, financial statements, business-report schedules, electronic record archives and retained supporting documents.
Decision Tree

The decision tree simplifies threshold questions that commonly determine the correct bookkeeping route. It is presented as a logical workflow so that the reader can follow the sequence as an operational progression rather than as a table of detached items.

  1. Identify the business event: sale, purchase, cash movement, payroll output, consumption-tax event, qualified invoice, electronic transaction, asset transaction, inventory movement, intercompany charge, adjustment or correction.
  2. Confirm whether the event belongs to a Japanese company, branch, permanent establishment or taxable activity subject to Japanese company, corporate-tax or consumption-tax requirements. If yes, proceed under Japanese requirements; if no, assess other jurisdictions or consolidation-only treatment.
  3. Check whether a reliable source document or electronic transaction record exists and whether it is sufficient to explain and substantiate the transaction. If not, resolve the documentation gap before recording.
  4. Assign the event to the appropriate accounts, determine the consumption-tax and corporate-tax treatment and record it through Japanese-yen double-entry accounting in the journal and general-ledger system.
  5. Assess whether the item has electronic-preservation, cross-border, foreign-currency, intercompany, transfer-pricing, group-reporting or tax-treaty elements. If yes, coordinate with Japanese accounting and tax professionals and group finance where necessary.
  6. Reconcile, preserve and retain the transaction, including its electronic evidence, and ensure it is correctly reflected in financial statements, tax returns, audit support and the applicable seven-year or ten-year retention process.

Cross-Border Relevance

Cross-border relevance explains why bookkeeping in Japan cannot be understood only as a domestic record-keeping process. International group structures, foreign ownership and multi-jurisdiction operations often trigger parallel bookkeeping questions.

Recognition Japanese bookkeeping obligations may arise where a Japanese company, foreign-owned subsidiary, branch, permanent establishment, consumption-tax registration, local taxable activity or other material business connection exists in Japan.
Foreign Companies Foreign-owned Japanese companies and foreign businesses with Japanese operations need accounting books that support the Companies Act, corporate tax, consumption tax, electronic record preservation and local financial-reporting requirements while allowing group reporting in other standards and currencies.
Applicable International Rules Bookkeeping may need to interface with Japanese GAAP, IFRS-based group reporting, foreign-currency translation, transfer pricing, customs and import-export records, tax treaty considerations and intercompany reporting, even though Japanese local obligations remain the baseline.
Language and Currency Considerations Local accounting books and tax records are commonly maintained in Japanese and Japanese yen. Foreign stakeholders commonly require English reporting packages, currency conversions and reconciliations, while foreign-language source documents must remain understandable and supportable for Japanese accounting and tax purposes.
Typical Cross-Border Scenario A foreign group establishes a Japanese K.K. or G.K., begins business through a branch or develops a Japanese taxable presence; local bookkeeping supports Japanese company, tax, consumption-tax and electronic-record obligations and is then reconciled to group accounts.
Common Risk Assuming that overseas group accounting, foreign invoices or ordinary cloud storage alone are sufficient, without addressing Japanese-yen books, local transaction evidence, electronic preservation, consumption-tax documentation and ten-year company-law retention.
Practical Consideration Cross-border bookkeeping often requires coordination between Japanese accountants, certified public tax accountants, group finance, tax advisers, auditors and IT or document-management teams to align local books, electronic evidence and international reporting.
Key Takeaways
  • Cross-border bookkeeping questions often begin when a foreign entity establishes a Japanese company, branch, permanent establishment or consumption-tax footprint.
  • Group accounting standards do not replace Japanese Companies Act books, corporate-tax records, electronic transaction preservation or consumption-tax documentation.
  • Coordination between Japanese local accounting processes and international group finance is essential for compliant reporting, tax support and reliable consolidation.
Operating Constraints Risks

Operating constraints identify the limits, risks and recurring friction points that affect bookkeeping execution in practice.

Documentation Risk Missing, incomplete or unreliable invoices, receipts, contracts, delivery records, payment evidence or inventory information can undermine accounting books, consumption-tax positions, financial statements and tax audit defence.
Electronic Preservation Risk Storing digital invoices, receipts, contracts and other electronic transaction data without controls for integrity, searchability, retrievability and appropriate preservation can create material tax-compliance risk under the electronic-books framework.
Retention Risk Applying only the seven-year corporate-tax period can be inadequate for stock companies because accounting books, important business materials and financial statements generally require ten-year retention under the Companies Act.
Cross-Border Risk Foreign-owned entities may underestimate Japanese requirements when relying mainly on overseas finance teams, group ledgers, foreign-language documents or systems not configured for Japanese-yen, consumption-tax and electronic-preservation controls.

FAQ

The FAQ section collects recurring threshold questions in a concise handbook format.

Must a Company Keep Accounting Books in Japan? Yes. A stock company prepares accounting books and important business materials in a timely manner. The books support financial statements, business reports, tax reporting, audit and company governance.
How Long Must Records Be Retained? Stock-company accounting books, important business materials, financial statements and supplementary schedules generally require ten-year retention. Corporate-tax books and documents generally follow seven years, with relevant loss-related records commonly requiring ten years.
Can Accounting Books Be Maintained Electronically? Yes. Accounting books and financial statements may be created and retained as electromagnetic records. Electronic transaction data must also meet the applicable electronic-books preservation and tax requirements for integrity, searchability and accessibility.
Does Bookkeeping Interact with Consumption Tax? Yes. Bookkeeping is the transaction-level foundation for consumption-tax classification, invoice and purchase evidence, tax calculation, filing and inspection support.
Can a Foreign Company Have Bookkeeping Obligations in Japan? Yes. Foreign-owned Japanese entities, branches, permanent establishments and businesses with Japanese taxable activity can have local company-law, corporate-tax, consumption-tax, payroll and electronic-record obligations.
