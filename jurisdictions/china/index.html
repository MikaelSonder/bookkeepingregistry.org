Executive Summary

Bookkeeping in China is the structured function that converts business events into reliable accounting vouchers, books, financial reports and accounting archives. The Accounting Law of the People’s Republic of China and the Accounting Standards for Business Enterprises (ASBE) form the core framework. Enterprises establish accounting books, prepare accounting vouchers, record transactions chronologically and systematically, and prepare financial reports that reflect the financial position, operating results and cash flows of the enterprise.

In professional practice, Chinese bookkeeping is not merely data entry. It is an integrated compliance process involving original-document validation, fapiao control, bookkeeping vouchers, journal and ledger posting, bank reconciliation, VAT and corporate income-tax accounting, payroll and social-insurance entries, asset and inventory controls, period closing and the preparation of financial reports for annual audit and regulatory reporting. Each recorded transaction requires sufficient documentary evidence and a traceable link between the underlying business event, accounting voucher and accounting book entry.

China’s fapiao system is a defining practical feature. A fapiao is an official tax invoice or receipt that supports revenue recognition, expense deductibility, input-VAT credit and tax compliance. Special VAT fapiao and other applicable invoice types are issued and managed through regulated tax systems, historically associated with the Golden Tax System. A bank transfer or internal reimbursement record does not by itself replace the need for valid fapiao or other legally sufficient source evidence for relevant business transactions.

Record retention in China is unusually long and highly document-specific. Under the Measures for the Administration of Accounting Archives, original and bookkeeping vouchers, general and subsidiary ledgers, journals and other principal accounting books are generally retained for thirty years. Monthly, quarterly and semiannual financial reports and bank statements are generally retained for ten years. Annual financial accounting reports are retained permanently. Tax books, accounting vouchers, statements, tax-payment certificates, invoices and other tax materials generally require at least ten years unless a more specific rule requires longer retention.

Purpose

The purpose of the bookkeeping function is to ensure that business transactions in China are recorded, documented and organised correctly, on time and in a way that supports accounting-law compliance, fapiao management, VAT and corporate-income-tax reporting, annual audit and reliable financial reports.

It exists to convert legal and commercial obligations into traceable accounting vouchers, books and archives with clear audit trails and predictable reporting outcomes.

Primary Outcome

Accurate and timely bookkeeping execution in China, including complete original and bookkeeping vouchers, reliable Chinese-language and RMB accounting books, compliant fapiao and tax evidence, support for VAT and corporate-income-tax reporting and robust input for financial reports and annual audit.

Country Characteristics

Country characteristics explain the jurisdiction-specific features that shape how bookkeeping operates in China. The section matters because bookkeeping is defined not only by arithmetic, but also by regulatory structure, documentation culture and institutional expectations.

Operational Culture Chinese bookkeeping is highly documentation-driven, formal and tax-integrated. Accounting vouchers, fapiao, books, tax filings, annual audit and accounting archives form an interconnected compliance chain.
Legal Framework Orientation The Accounting Law and Accounting Standards for Business Enterprises establish the core accounting framework. Tax administration, fapiao regulation, VAT, corporate-income-tax rules and the Measures for the Administration of Accounting Archives create additional operational requirements.
Language and Currency Requirement Statutory accounting books and financial reports are maintained in Chinese. RMB is the required presentation currency for financial reports. Enterprises using foreign currencies in their business transactions may use an approved foreign-currency bookkeeping base, but statutory financial reports remain presented in RMB.
Archive Retention Characteristic China applies long, detailed accounting-archive periods: thirty years for core vouchers and accounting books, ten years for periodic financial reports and bank records and permanent retention for annual financial accounting reports. These periods exceed those in most comparable jurisdictions.

Key Takeaways
  • Chinese bookkeeping is strongly shaped by the Accounting Law, ASBE, formal accounting vouchers, fapiao and Ministry of Finance archive rules.
  • Chinese-language and RMB statutory records, tax-valid fapiao and traceable voucher-to-ledger controls are central practical requirements.
  • Foreign-invested enterprises must maintain Chinese-compliant books and archives even if group accounting, finance teams and reporting systems operate abroad.
Regulatory & Operational Framework

The regulatory and operational framework identifies the principal rule layers that define Chinese bookkeeping practice. The section is broader than legislation alone because bookkeeping depends on accounting law, tax administration, fapiao controls, archive rules, annual audit and operational procedures.

Accounting Law of the People’s Republic of China Establishes the core framework for accounting organisation, accounting books, accounting vouchers, financial reports, accounting personnel, internal controls and legal responsibility for accounting compliance by entities operating in China.
Accounting Standards for Business Enterprises (ASBE) Provides the principal accounting standards framework for enterprises. ASBE is substantially converged with IFRS in many areas but remains a separate Chinese statutory framework with local application, reporting and implementation requirements.
Fapiao and Golden Tax System Fapiao are official tax invoices or receipts forming a core evidence layer for revenue, expenses, VAT and tax compliance. Applicable invoice issuance, receipt, verification and management occur through regulated tax-administration systems associated with the Golden Tax infrastructure.
VAT and Corporate Income Tax Records VAT, corporate income tax and other tax compliance rely on accounting books, vouchers, fapiao, tax payment certificates, returns and supporting documents. Proper bookkeeping provides the basis for periodic filing, annual tax settlement and tax inspection support.
Annual Audit and Financial Reports Foreign-invested enterprises and many other enterprise categories prepare annual financial reports and are subject to annual audit requirements under the applicable Chinese legal and regulatory framework. Financial reports are prepared in Chinese and presented in RMB for statutory purposes.
Measures for the Administration of Accounting Archives Establishes document-specific archiving requirements for original vouchers, bookkeeping vouchers, journals, general and subsidiary ledgers, financial reports, bank records, tax materials and electronic accounting archives. Principal vouchers and books generally require thirty years; annual financial reports are retained permanently.
Process Flow

The process flow explains how bookkeeping work usually progresses from raw transaction to completed records and reporting support. It matters because bookkeeping is an operating sequence, not a single event.

1. Original Document and Fapiao Collection Collect issued and received fapiao, invoices, receipts, bank statements, contracts, delivery records, payroll outputs, VAT evidence, asset records, inventory information and other original documents for each business transaction.
2. Validation and Classification Validate the original document, assess fapiao status and tax relevance, classify sales, purchases, expenses, payroll, assets, liabilities and other events to appropriate accounts and determine their VAT, corporate-income-tax and ASBE treatment.
3. Bookkeeping Voucher Preparation Prepare a bookkeeping voucher for the transaction, with clear narration, account coding, original-document references and sufficient supporting evidence to create a traceable audit trail.
4. Journal and Ledger Posting Record transactions chronologically and systematically in the journal, general ledger and subsidiary ledgers, maintaining Chinese-language accounting records and appropriate RMB reporting treatment.
5. Tax, Fapiao and Bank Reconciliation Reconcile bank accounts, receivables, payables, VAT input and output records, fapiao data, corporate-income-tax schedules, payroll and social-insurance liabilities, assets, inventory and other material balances.
6. Period Closing and Filing Support Perform period-end procedures and adjustment entries, prepare reconciled data for VAT, corporate income tax, payroll and other tax filings and support management reporting and tax authority information requests.
7. Financial Reports, Audit and Archiving Prepare financial reports and annual audit schedules where required, organise vouchers and books into compliant accounting archives and transfer records to archive management in accordance with the applicable retention schedule.
Typical Outputs Original vouchers, bookkeeping vouchers, journal, general ledger, subsidiary ledgers, trial balance, fapiao records, VAT workpapers, bank reconciliations, asset and inventory records, financial reports, audit schedules and archived accounting files.
Decision Tree

The decision tree simplifies threshold questions that commonly determine the correct bookkeeping route. It is presented as a logical workflow so that the reader can follow the sequence as an operational progression rather than as a table of detached items.

  1. Identify the business event: sale, purchase, cash movement, payroll output, VAT event, fapiao issue or receipt, asset transaction, inventory movement, import-export transaction, intercompany charge, adjustment or correction.
  2. Confirm whether the event belongs to a Chinese enterprise, WFOE, joint venture, branch, permanent establishment or taxable activity subject to Chinese accounting, tax or VAT requirements. If yes, proceed under Chinese requirements; if no, assess other jurisdictions or consolidation-only treatment.
  3. Check whether valid original documentation and, where relevant, a compliant fapiao exists and is sufficient to explain and substantiate the transaction. If not, resolve the documentation or fapiao gap before recording.
  4. Determine the appropriate ASBE account classification, VAT and corporate-income-tax treatment and prepare the bookkeeping voucher before posting the transaction to journals and ledgers.
  5. Assess whether the item has fapiao, Golden Tax, cross-border, foreign-currency, intercompany, transfer-pricing, customs, annual-audit or group-reporting elements. If yes, coordinate with Chinese accounting and tax professionals and group finance where necessary.
  6. Reconcile, archive and retain the transaction and its voucher trail, ensuring it is reflected correctly in tax filings, financial reports, annual audit support and the applicable ten-year, thirty-year or permanent archive category.

Cross-Border Relevance

Cross-border relevance explains why bookkeeping in China cannot be understood only as a domestic record-keeping process. International group structures, foreign ownership and multi-jurisdiction operations often trigger parallel bookkeeping questions.

Recognition Chinese bookkeeping obligations may arise where a Chinese company, WFOE, joint venture, branch, permanent establishment, tax registration, VAT activity, import-export operation or other material business connection exists in mainland China.
Foreign-Invested Enterprises Foreign-invested enterprises and foreign businesses with Chinese operations need accounting books that support the Accounting Law, ASBE, fapiao, VAT, corporate-income-tax, annual-audit and accounting-archive obligations while allowing group reporting in other standards and currencies.
Applicable International Rules Bookkeeping may need to interface with ASBE, IFRS-based or US-GAAP group reporting, foreign-currency translation, transfer pricing, customs and import-export records, tax treaty considerations and intercompany reporting, even though Chinese statutory accounting and tax obligations remain the local baseline.
Language and Currency Considerations Statutory accounting records are maintained in Chinese and statutory financial reports are presented in RMB. Foreign-invested enterprises may maintain a parallel foreign-language or foreign-currency management layer, but it does not replace Chinese statutory books, fapiao controls or RMB reporting.
Typical Cross-Border Scenario A foreign group establishes a WFOE, joint venture, branch or taxable presence in China; local bookkeeping supports Chinese accounting, fapiao, VAT, corporate-income-tax, annual-audit and archive obligations and is then reconciled to group accounts.
Common Risk Assuming that overseas group accounting, foreign invoices, bank transfers or ordinary cloud storage alone are sufficient, without addressing Chinese-language books, RMB reporting, valid fapiao, local vouchers, tax records, annual audit and thirty-year core archive retention.
Practical Consideration Cross-border bookkeeping often requires coordination between Chinese accountants, tax advisers, certified public accountants, fapiao administrators, group finance, customs teams and auditors to align local statutory books, tax evidence and international reporting.
Key Takeaways
  • Cross-border bookkeeping questions often begin when a foreign entity establishes a WFOE, joint venture, branch, permanent establishment or tax footprint in China.
  • Group accounting standards do not replace Chinese Accounting Law records, Chinese-language RMB books, fapiao controls, local vouchers, tax evidence, annual audit or accounting-archive obligations.
  • Coordination between Chinese local accounting processes and international group finance is essential for compliant reporting, tax support, annual audit and reliable consolidation.
Operating Constraints Risks

Operating constraints identify the limits, risks and recurring friction points that affect bookkeeping execution in practice.

Fapiao and Documentation Risk Missing, invalid or mismatched fapiao, incomplete original documents, unreliable contracts, absent delivery evidence or unsupported payment records can undermine expense deduction, input-VAT credit, accounting books, tax filings and audit defence.
Language and Currency Risk Maintaining only foreign-language or foreign-currency group records can fail to meet Chinese statutory book and RMB financial-report requirements. Parallel group records do not replace the local accounting and voucher trail.
Archive Retention Risk Applying a generic ten-year group-retention policy can be inadequate because principal vouchers, journals and ledgers generally require thirty-year retention and annual financial reports require permanent retention under the accounting-archive framework.
Cross-Border Risk Foreign-invested enterprises may underestimate Chinese requirements when relying mainly on overseas finance teams, group ledgers, foreign invoices or systems not configured for fapiao, Chinese documentation, RMB reporting, local tax filings and annual audit.

FAQ

The FAQ section collects recurring threshold questions in a concise handbook format.

Must an Enterprise Keep Accounting Records in China? Yes. Enterprises maintain accounting books, vouchers, financial reports and other accounting records under the Accounting Law and the Accounting Standards for Business Enterprises framework.
In Which Language and Currency Are Records Maintained? Statutory accounting records are maintained in Chinese and financial reports are presented in RMB. A permitted foreign-currency bookkeeping base may be used for relevant business activity, but it does not replace RMB statutory reporting.
Why Is Fapiao Important? Fapiao is a core official tax-invoice or receipt mechanism. It supports revenue, expense deduction, input-VAT credit, tax compliance and the evidential link between a business event, accounting voucher and accounting book entry.
How Long Must Records Be Retained? Original and bookkeeping vouchers, journals, general ledgers, subsidiary ledgers and other principal books generally require thirty-year retention. Periodic financial reports and bank statements generally require ten years, while annual financial accounting reports are retained permanently.
Can a Foreign Company Have Bookkeeping Obligations in China? Yes. Foreign-invested enterprises, WFOEs, joint ventures, branches, permanent establishments and businesses with Chinese tax or VAT activity can have local accounting, fapiao, tax, annual-audit, financial-reporting and archive obligations.
