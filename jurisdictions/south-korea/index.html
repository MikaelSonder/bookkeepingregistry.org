Executive Summary

Bookkeeping in South Korea is the structured function that converts business events into reliable trade books, accounting records, supporting documents and financial statements. Under the Korean Commercial Act, merchants maintain trade books and important business documents. South Korean companies use double-entry bookkeeping to record business transactions and prepare annual financial statements that present the financial position, performance, cash flows and changes in equity of the company.

In professional practice, South Korean bookkeeping is not merely data entry. It is an ongoing compliance process involving invoices and receipt control, sales and purchase recording, journal and general-ledger maintenance, VAT tax-invoice management, bank reconciliation, payroll and social-insurance accounting, asset and inventory controls, period closing and preparation of financial statements under the applicable Korean reporting framework.

South Korea operates a differentiated reporting environment. Listed companies and financial institutions generally apply Korean International Financial Reporting Standards (K-IFRS). Other non-listed companies generally apply Korean Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (K-GAAP), also referred to as accounting standards for non-public entities, or SME Accounting Standards where applicable. Foreign-owned subsidiaries may also prepare reconciliation packages for a parent applying IFRS, US GAAP or another group framework.

Retention is document-specific. Under the Commercial Act, trade books and important business documents are generally preserved for ten years, while documents supporting book entries are generally retained for five years. Corporate Tax Act documentary evidence is generally retained for five years, extending to seven years for cross-border transactions. VAT tax invoices and related records also generally follow a five-year retention period. Local accounting and tax records are commonly maintained in Korean and Korean won, with cross-border groups requiring coordinated translation and currency-reconciliation controls.

Purpose

The purpose of the bookkeeping function is to ensure that business transactions in South Korea are recorded, documented and organised correctly, on time and in a way that supports Korean Commercial Act compliance, NTS tax and VAT reporting, reliable financial statements and transparent business administration.

It exists to convert legal and commercial obligations into traceable trade books and accounting records with clear audit trails and predictable reporting outcomes.

Primary Outcome

Accurate and timely bookkeeping execution in South Korea, including complete supporting documents, reliable trade books and ledgers, compliant Korean-language and KRW records, support for VAT and tax declarations and robust input for K-IFRS, K-GAAP or SME-standard financial statements.

Regulatory & Operational Framework

The regulatory and operational framework identifies the principal rule layers that define South Korean bookkeeping practice. The section is broader than legislation alone because bookkeeping depends on commercial law, tax and VAT rules, accounting standards, documentation routines and operational procedures.

Korean Commercial Act Requires merchants to maintain trade books and preserve trade books and important business documents for ten years. Documents supporting entries in trade books are generally preserved for five years. The Commercial Act provides the core business-record foundation for companies and merchants.
Korean Accounting Standards K-IFRS is generally mandatory for listed companies and financial institutions. Other companies apply K-GAAP or SME Accounting Standards as applicable. Proper bookkeeping provides the transaction-level basis for the balance sheet, income statement, statement of changes in equity, cash-flow statement and notes.
Corporate Tax Act Record Requirements Corporations prepare or receive documentary evidence for business-related transactions and retain it for generally five years from the relevant reporting deadline. Cross-border transaction documentation may require a seven-year retention period.
Value-Added Tax and Tax Invoices VAT-registered businesses maintain sales and purchase information, tax invoices and supporting records that establish taxable supplies, input tax and output tax. Electronic tax invoices and NTS reporting are key operational components of South Korean VAT compliance.
Financial Statements and Audit Companies prepare annual financial statements under the applicable Korean accounting framework. Statutory external-audit requirements depend on entity type, size and public-accountability factors; bookkeeping records must be sufficient to support financial reporting and audit where required.
Record Storage and Retention Retention is document-specific: trade books and important business documents generally require ten years; supporting documents, VAT evidence and many corporate-tax records generally require five years; and relevant cross-border corporate-tax documents may require seven years. Entities should apply the longest relevant period for each record category.
Process Flow

The process flow explains how bookkeeping work usually progresses from raw transaction to completed records and reporting support. It matters because bookkeeping is an operating sequence, not a single event.

1. Source Document Collection Collect issued and received tax invoices, receipts, bank statements, contracts, delivery records, payroll outputs, VAT evidence, asset records, inventory information and other supporting documents for each business event.
2. Classification and VAT Review Classify sales, purchases, expenses, payroll, assets, liabilities and other events to appropriate accounts and determine their VAT, corporate-tax, accounting-standard and financial-reporting treatment where applicable.
3. Journal Entry Record business events chronologically in journals using double-entry accounting, with clear descriptions, account coding, tax-invoice references and links to reliable supporting evidence.
4. General and Subsidiary Ledger Posting Post entries systematically to the general ledger and maintain subsidiary ledgers for receivables, payables, cash, banks, fixed assets, inventory, payroll, VAT and intercompany balances.
5. VAT Tax-Invoice and NTS Control Issue, receive, validate and reconcile VAT tax invoices and relevant sales and purchase information, ensuring bookkeeping records support NTS electronic reporting and VAT-return preparation.
6. Reconciliation and Period Closing Reconcile bank accounts, receivables, payables, VAT accounts, payroll and social-insurance liabilities, assets, inventory and other material balances; then complete period-end adjustment entries.
7. Financial Statements and Tax Support Provide final figures, schedules, trade books and supporting materials for financial statements, corporate-tax and VAT filings, statutory audit and NTS review where applicable.
Typical Outputs Trade books, journal, general ledger, subsidiary ledgers, trial balance, VAT tax-invoice records, bank reconciliations, asset and inventory registers, financial-statement schedules and archived supporting documents.
Decision Tree

The decision tree simplifies threshold questions that commonly determine the correct bookkeeping route. It is presented as a logical workflow so that the reader can follow the sequence as an operational progression rather than as a table of detached items.

  1. Identify the business event: sale, purchase, cash movement, payroll output, VAT tax-invoice event, asset transaction, inventory movement, intercompany charge, adjustment or correction.
  2. Confirm whether the event belongs to a South Korean company, branch, permanent establishment or taxable activity subject to Korean commercial, corporate-tax or VAT requirements. If yes, proceed under South Korean requirements; if no, assess other jurisdictions or consolidation-only treatment.
  3. Check whether a valid tax invoice, receipt, contract, bank record or other supporting document exists and is sufficient to explain the transaction. If not, resolve the documentation gap before recording.
  4. Assign the event to the appropriate accounts, determine the VAT and tax treatment and record it through double-entry accounting in Korean won in the trade-book and general-ledger system.
  5. Assess whether the item has cross-border, foreign-currency, intercompany, transfer-pricing, group-reporting or seven-year tax-documentation elements. If yes, coordinate with Korean accountants, tax advisers and group finance where necessary.
  6. Reconcile and retain the transaction, include it correctly in VAT, tax, financial-statement and audit processes and apply the relevant five-year, seven-year or ten-year retention category.

Cross-Border Relevance

Cross-border relevance explains why bookkeeping in South Korea cannot be understood only as a domestic record-keeping process. International group structures, foreign ownership and multi-jurisdiction operations often trigger parallel bookkeeping questions.

Recognition South Korean bookkeeping obligations may arise where a Korean company, foreign-owned subsidiary, branch, permanent establishment, VAT registration, local taxable activity or other material business connection exists in South Korea.
Foreign Companies Foreign-owned Korean companies and foreign businesses with South Korean operations need trade books and accounting records that support Korean Commercial Act, NTS corporate-tax and VAT compliance and applicable K-IFRS or K-GAAP reporting while allowing group reporting in other standards and currencies.
Applicable International Rules Bookkeeping may need to interface with K-IFRS, IFRS-based or US-GAAP group reporting, foreign-currency translation, transfer pricing, tax treaty considerations, customs records and cross-border tax documentation, even though South Korean local obligations remain the baseline.
Language and Currency Considerations Local financial statements are prepared in Korean and Korean won. Foreign stakeholders commonly require English reporting packages, foreign-currency translation and reconciliations, while foreign-language source documents must remain supportable for Korean accounting, tax and audit purposes.
Typical Cross-Border Scenario A foreign group establishes a South Korean stock company or limited company, begins business through a branch or develops a Korean taxable presence; local bookkeeping supports Korean commercial, tax, VAT and financial-reporting obligations and is then reconciled to group accounts.
Common Risk Assuming that overseas group accounting, foreign invoices or a non-Korean chart of accounts alone are sufficient, without addressing Korean-won books, VAT tax invoices, NTS documentation, local standards and the applicable five-year, seven-year or ten-year retention rules.
Practical Consideration Cross-border bookkeeping often requires coordination between South Korean accountants, tax advisers, group finance, VAT specialists, auditors and payroll teams to align local books, tax evidence, financial statements and international reporting.
Key Takeaways
  • Cross-border bookkeeping questions often begin when a foreign entity establishes a South Korean company, branch, permanent establishment or local VAT footprint.
  • Group accounting standards do not replace Korean Commercial Act trade books, NTS tax records, VAT tax-invoice evidence, K-IFRS or K-GAAP reporting and local retention obligations.
  • Coordination between South Korean local accounting processes and international group finance is essential for compliant reporting, tax support and reliable consolidation.
Operating Constraints Risks

Operating constraints identify the limits, risks and recurring friction points that affect bookkeeping execution in practice.

Documentation Risk Missing, incomplete or unreliable tax invoices, receipts, contracts, payment evidence or inventory information can undermine trade books, VAT positions, financial statements, corporate-tax support and audit defence.
VAT Tax-Invoice Risk Incorrect VAT classification, missing or invalid tax invoices, incomplete sales and purchase records or failures to reconcile tax-invoice data with the general ledger can create reporting inconsistencies and NTS compliance risk.
Retention Risk Applying only a five-year tax or VAT period can be inadequate because trade books and important business documents generally require ten-year preservation, while relevant cross-border corporate-tax documentation may require seven years.
Cross-Border Risk Foreign-owned entities may underestimate South Korean requirements when relying mainly on overseas finance teams, group ledgers, foreign-language documents or systems not configured for Korean-language, KRW, NTS VAT and local reporting controls.

FAQ

The FAQ section collects recurring threshold questions in a concise handbook format.

Must a Company Keep Accounting Records in South Korea? Yes. Merchants maintain trade books and important business documents under the Commercial Act, and companies maintain accounting records and prepare financial statements under the applicable Korean legal and accounting framework.
Which Accounting Standards Apply? Listed companies and financial institutions generally apply K-IFRS. Other companies generally apply K-GAAP or SME Accounting Standards, with the applicable framework determined by the entity’s characteristics and reporting obligations.
How Long Must Records Be Retained? Trade books and important business documents generally require ten-year retention. Supporting documents, VAT evidence and most corporate-tax documentation generally follow five years, while relevant cross-border tax documentation may require seven years.
Does Bookkeeping Interact with VAT Tax Invoices? Yes. Bookkeeping is the transaction-level foundation for VAT tax invoices, sales and purchase evidence, VAT calculations, National Tax Service reporting, filing and tax-audit support.
Can a Foreign Company Have Bookkeeping Obligations in South Korea? Yes. Foreign-owned South Korean entities, branches, permanent establishments and businesses with South Korean VAT or taxable activity can have local accounting, tax, VAT tax-invoice, payroll, financial-statement and record-retention obligations.
